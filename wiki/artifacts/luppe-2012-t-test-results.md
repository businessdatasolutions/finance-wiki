# Table 4 — t-test results of transformed estimates

> Per-company t-statistics comparing the low-anchor and high-anchor groups' transformed estimates. All 8 tests reach p < 0.01 with N = 79 — anchoring is **highly statistically significant** across every accounting-magnitude question tested. The two highest t-statistics (Apple 11.46; Sears 9.73) align with the two extreme AI values in [[luppe-2012-anchoring-index-results]] (Table 2): Apple AI = 1.38, Sears AI = 0.75 with strong low-anchor effect.

## Reading the table

- **Apple Computer (t = 11.46)** is the largest effect — both because the anchoring index is the highest (AI = 1.38) and because the variance within each anchor group was tight enough to produce a large t-statistic. Apple's profitability is genuinely uncertain to non-finance Brazilian student respondents, so anchors are maximally informative.
- **Sears (t = 9.73)** is second largest. Sears was a more obscure name to the respondent pool than the other US companies — anchor uptake was high because respondents had little prior to anchor against.
- **Grupo Pão de Açúcar (t = 9.42)** is the third — domestic Brazilian retailer; anchored estimates also showed AI = 1.36 (the second-highest in the sample). Same dynamic as Apple — high uncertainty, strong anchor uptake.
- **CVRD (t = 6.34)** is the smallest. CVRD's calibration-group median (R$ 900 M) was close to the low anchor (R$ 750 M), so the low-anchor effect was weak (Low AI = 0.11). The high-anchor effect drove the t-statistic.

## What the table validates

The combination of (a) all t-statistics > 6, (b) all p-values < 0.01, and (c) N = 79 per company makes a strong case that anchoring is **not a marginal-significance effect** in this experimental context. It is robust, reproducible across companies, and large in magnitude.

The point-biserial correlation reported in source page §Results (mean = 0.13) is much smaller — it measures a different thing (correlation between "estimate above or below anchor" and the value), not the t-test of medians.
