# How The Best Companies Defend Against Mediocrity And Rot

> In this episode of *The Main Function*, Garry sits down with Eric Ries, author of *The Lean Startup*, about his new book *Incorruptible: Why Good Companies Go Bad And How Great Companies Stay Great*. Ries breaks down why shareholder primacy often leads to company and product degradation, how founders can lose control of the companies they build, and what legal structures and governance models can protect a company's core mission from outside threats.

## TL;DR

Eric Ries — author of *The Lean Startup* (2011) — releases a new book *Incorruptible* and frames its central argument: **shareholder primacy is not a law; it is a doctrine**, and companies that take it seriously systematically degrade their products and missions over time. The book's solution is **governance structure**: Public Benefit Corporation status (a two-page Delaware filing), mission-controlled boards (Anthropic's long-term-benefit trust), industrial foundations (Novo Nordisk's Foundation owns ~28% with majority voting), and dual-class shares (better than nothing, but insufficient on its own). Live examples: Sol Price → Costco; Patagonia; Anthropic; Novo Nordisk. Pre-order the book.

## Context (WHY)

Eric Ries is the author of *The Lean Startup* (2011), one of the most influential startup-methodology books of the 2010s. *Incorruptible* is his return to long-form writing 15 years later. The book's thesis (per this interview and the sibling Lenny's Podcast episode):

- **"Financial gravity"** — a force that drags successful organisations down toward mediocrity. Ries calls it "the force that no one controls but everyone obeys."
- **Shareholder primacy as the mechanism.** Delaware's Common Code does not actually mandate shareholder-value maximisation; the doctrine was invented and propagated through 20th-century business education (Milton Friedman, Jensen-Meckling agency theory) and is now treated as if it were statute.
- **Founder ouster as the symptom.** "80% of venture-backed founders are ousted within three years of going public" (per the description); the book argues this is structurally caused by the governance setup, not by individual founder failure.
- **Mission protection through governance** as the solution — covered in detail under [[#Distinctive artifacts]].

Ries names live examples: Twilio / Jeff Lawson (founder removed by board), Apple / Steve Jobs (1985 ouster), Sol Price / Costco (mission preserved via fiduciary-to-customer ethos), Patagonia (Yvon Chouinard's 2022 transfer to a trust), Anthropic (long-term-benefit trust), Novo Nordisk (foundation control reaching ~$600 B valuation).

## Results (WHAT)

What Ries argues. The most-citable specific claims:

- **Delaware General Corporation Law does NOT contain "shareholder primacy"**. Ries: *"If you look at the 19th century like companies that were created and you read their charter, none of them say maximize shareholder [value]."* The doctrine is read into "fiduciary duty" by convention, not by statute.
- **The Public Benefit Corporation (PBC) filing is two pages**. A simple amendment to a Delaware C-Corp that requires the board to consider the public-benefit purpose written into the charter. Pre-funding stage: nearly free; post-funding stage: requires investor consent.
- **Mission-Controlled Companies** = a class of structures (PBC, trust-controlled, foundation-controlled, dual-class) that bind future boards and management to a founding mission via legal mechanism rather than aspirational mission statement.
- **The Anthropic governance structure** — directors on the for-profit board appointed by an outside group of AI-safety-expert trustees who do not have equity. Stated by Ries as the reason Anthropic can refuse to ship dangerous models without shareholder revolt.
- **The Novo Nordisk model** — the Novo Nordisk Foundation owns ~28% of shares with majority voting rights; the company's valuation reached ~$600 B in the Ozempic-driven 2023-2024 surge. Industrial foundations outperform listed peers in aggregate, per studies Ries references but does not cite by name in this interview.
- **Dual-class shares are insufficient on their own.** Better than nothing but still allows founder-or-successor to drift from the mission once equity stakes dilute.

### Ries's four mission-protection mechanisms

**Type:** named governance framework · **Location:** scattered across chapters 5, 8–10, 14, 17–18 · **Reproduced in:** [[ries-2026-four-mission-protection-mechanisms]]

The four governance structures Ries argues protect a company from financial-gravity erosion of its mission: Public Benefit Corporation (PBC), Mission-Controlled board, Industrial Foundation, Dual-Class shares. Each with strengths, weaknesses, and the live cases (Anthropic, Costco/Patagonia, Novo Nordisk, etc.).

### The Anthropic governance case (inline — short)

```
Anthropic's structure:
  For-profit C-Corp board
       ↓ directors appointed and accountable to ↓
  Long-Term Benefit Trust
       ↓ trustees are AI safety experts ↓
  Trustees hold NO equity in Anthropic

Mechanism: when commercial pressure conflicts with safety mission,
  trust-appointed directors have a fiduciary obligation to the safety
  mission, not to shareholder return. Equity-free trustees means no
  personal-wealth incentive to override safety.
```

Ries names this as the cleanest current example of mission protection — *"Whenever you see Anthropic do the right thing, like when they refuse to release a model because they think it's too dangerous, think about how much that's costing them. The reason they're able to do that is because they wrote into their charter [this structure]."*

**Limitations the source acknowledges:**

- Book-launch interview — promotional intent. Ries is selling pre-orders.
- The "80% of founders ousted within 3 years of IPO" stat is asserted; not cited to a specific study in this interview.

**Limitations not flagged:**

- **Survivorship bias.** Costco, Patagonia, Anthropic, Novo Nordisk are presented as exemplary; their successful navigation of the governance challenge is part of the case. Companies that adopted similar governance and still failed are not surveyed in the interview.
- **Founder-control bias.** Ries's audience (YC, Lenny's) is founder-aligned; the framework valorises founder mission against board / shareholder discipline. A symmetric case exists: founders who genuinely should have been removed (cult-leader founders, fraud founders, drift-from-strategy founders). The interview does not engage that case.
- **Industrial-foundation governance is statutorily different across jurisdictions.** Novo Nordisk is Danish; Patagonia's structure is US-specific; UK-, German-, Dutch- equivalents would require different legal mechanics. The interview presents the model as portable; portability is a question.
- **"Shareholder primacy is not a law" is true but elides the case law.** While Delaware GCL doesn't mandate it as statute, Revlon-doctrine and similar cases create real-world constraints on board discretion. The interview's "it's not even a law" framing is rhetorically strong but legally simplified.
