## Context (WHY)

**System dynamics** is a modelling methodology developed by **Jay W. Forrester** at MIT starting in the 1950s — originally to model industrial supply chains (the "Forrester effect" / bullwhip in supply chains), then extended to urban dynamics, world resources (the *Limits to Growth* report, 1972), and now broadly to complex social-economic systems. The methodology centres on **stocks and flows** (state variables and their rates of change) plus **feedback loops** (reinforcing and balancing) plus **delays** (the structural source of overshoot and policy resistance).

John Sterman is Forrester's most prominent successor at MIT — he holds the chair named after Forrester (the "JW Forrester Professor of Management"). His textbook *Business Dynamics: Systems Thinking and Modeling for a Complex World* (2000) is the field's standard reference. He has also led MIT's research on climate-policy misperceptions and developed widely-used educational simulations (the Beer Game in particular).

The 2026 webinar appears to be an executive-education recruiting / public-introduction event — MIT Sloan Executive Education offers paid courses on system dynamics; this free webinar serves as a tasting-menu introduction.

## Methods (HOW)

System dynamics methodology, as Sterman teaches it:

1. **Identify the problem dynamically** — what variables matter, and how have they evolved over time? (Behaviour-over-time graphs as the starting artifact.)
2. **Hypothesise causal structure** — stocks, flows, feedback loops, and delays that *could* produce the observed behaviour.
3. **Build a quantitative model** — typically in Stella, Vensim, or similar SD modelling tools.
4. **Test against history** — does the model reproduce the historical behaviour? If not, the structural hypothesis is wrong.
5. **Test policies** — what interventions in the modelled system produce the desired behaviour? Where are the high-leverage points?
6. **Communicate causal loop diagrams** — the qualitative visual representation that lets non-modellers reason about the structure.

The webinar would have introduced these elements in a 60-minute compressed form; rigorous practice requires the longer course.

## Results (WHAT)

The webinar's main pedagogical points (inferred from the description and the segment samples; not exhaustively transcribed):

### Motivation: unintended consequences

> *"In today's fast-changing and highly interconnected world, many of the most persistent challenges leaders face arise from unintended consequences of earlier decisions. Actions that succeed in the short run often create new risks and long-term failure."*

This is the canonical systems-thinking motivation — short-run solutions create long-run problems, and the failure pattern is structurally predictable from the system's feedback structure.

### Slogan vs practice

Sterman's repeated argument across his work: "systems thinking" is widely invoked but rarely practised. Practical use requires moving from intuitive systems-language to formal modelling — stock-and-flow diagrams, causal loop diagrams, quantitative simulation. The webinar's pitch is that this move is **a learnable executive capability**, not just an academic technique.

### Classic teaching artifacts (likely covered)

Sterman's standard pedagogical canon (from *Business Dynamics* and his lectures):

- **The bathtub-stock fallacy** — most people fail elementary stock-and-flow reasoning when asked about, e.g., CO2 accumulation vs CO2 emission rates.
- **The Beer Game** — classroom supply-chain simulation that reliably produces bullwhip oscillation regardless of who plays.
- **Climate-policy misperception experiments** — Sterman's published work showing that even highly-educated audiences misjudge climate-policy outcomes because they don't internalise stock-accumulation logic.
- **Worse-before-better dynamics** — policies that genuinely solve long-run problems often look like they're making things worse in the short run (delay structures).

### Leverage points

Drawing on Donella Meadows's *Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System* (1999), Sterman emphasises that high-leverage interventions are usually in places that intuition misses — system goals, paradigms, and feedback-loop structures, rather than parameters or buffer-stocks (the places executives habitually intervene).

**Limitations the source acknowledges:** webinar-length pedagogy compresses years of methodology into 60 minutes; the speaker repeatedly notes the longer course is needed for genuine capability.

**Limitations not flagged:**

- **System dynamics is methodologically demanding.** The webinar's "leaders can use this" framing oversells how quickly executives can develop competence; rigorous SD modelling requires significant training that 60 minutes cannot substitute for.
- **The methodology has been around for 70 years.** Its slow adoption in mainstream management is itself a systems-dynamics phenomenon worth Sterman explicitly addressing — why have organisations consistently failed to adopt the methodology despite its demonstrated value? (Not engaged in the webinar.)
- **Quantitative SD modelling requires software + data.** The slogan-vs-practice framing is correct but the practical step from slogans to models is non-trivial — Stella / Vensim licensing, time-series data collection, model validation cycles all add real cost. The webinar undersells this gap.
