Abstract

We synthesise the empirical literature on the determinants and consequences of
ﬁnancial distress, critique the ﬁndings and oﬀer suggestions for future research.
We categorise these indicators into (i) ﬁrm-level fundamental determinants, (ii)
macroeconomic determinants and (iii) ﬁrm-level corporate governance deter-
minants. We categorise the consequences into (i) ﬁnancial reporting and
auditing consequences, (ii) ﬁrm-level operational consequences, (iii) capital
market consequences and (iv) corporate governance consequences. We suggest
that future research can make a more meaningful contribution, by developing
more comprehensive models of predicting ﬁnancial distress which will entail a
departure from the current partial analysis to a more holistic complex analysis.

Key words: Financial distress; Bankruptcies; Corporate governance; Financial
reporting; Capital markets

1. Introduction

   The purpose of this paper was to synthesise the vast body of empirical
literature on the determinants and consequences of ﬁrm-speciﬁc ﬁnancial
distress. Corporate ﬁnancial distress encompasses four generic terms: failure,
insolvency, bankruptcy and default. Failure occurs when the realised rate of
return on investments, after adjusting for risk considerations, is signiﬁcantly
lower than the returns generated from comparable investments, or when
revenue is insuﬃcient to cover costs. Insolvency refers to ﬁrms’ inability to

indicates that the ﬁrm is in ﬁnancial distress, which in most jurisdictions
requires a legal declaration involving the courts. Default can be both technical
and legal. Technical default refers to the situation where a ﬁrm breached a
condition stipulated in contracts (e.g. debt covenant violation). Failure to meet
periodic repayments on a loan is more likely to lead to a legal default.
Irrespective of the nature of the default, both types of default signal a
deteriorating ﬁrm performance and ﬁnancial distress (Altman and Hotchkiss,
2010). Although ﬁnancial distress does not necessarily imply that the distressed
ﬁrms will ultimately fail, a signiﬁcant and persistent decline in a company’s
ﬁnancial performance may eventually result in bankruptcy, making investors
and creditors suﬀer considerable ﬁnancial loss.
   Stakeholders, therefore, need to know potential indicators of impending
distress so that they can protect their interests. A number of possible indicators
have been proposed and tested in the literature. These encompass ﬁrm-level
fundamental determinants (e.g. corporate hedging policies; employee relations;
management narrative disclosures; and corporate social responsibility activi-
ties), macroeconomic determinants (e.g. business cycle and country risk factors)
and ﬁrm-level corporate governance determinants (e.g. board structure,
ownership composition and management quality). Before we review this
strand of the literature, we begin our review by summarising the distress
measures used in the extant research. This is justiﬁed, as the validity of the
reported ﬁndings on ﬁnancial distress hinges critically on the reliability of the
measurement techniques applied.
   Finally, we review the consequences of ﬁnancial distress, categorised into (i)
ﬁnancial reporting and auditing consequences; (ii) ﬁrm-level operational conse-
quences; (iii) capital market consequences; and (iv) corporate governance conse-
quences. Financial reporting provides the primary source of independently veriﬁed
information to capital providers about the performance of managers (Sloan, 2001).
Hence, ﬁnancial reporting transparency for ﬁrms approaching ﬁnancial distress
should provide an early warning signal about the impending problem. Firm-level
operational consequences include distressed ﬁrms’ decisions on adjusting trade
credit/trade receivables and tax avoidance. Capital market consequences of
ﬁnancial distress include dividend adjustments, and research on the ability of
distress risk to explain some of the accounting and market anomalies. This stream of
research generally concludes that distress risk can predict the abnormal returns of
the accrual strategy and return momentum. Finally, research on the corporate
governance consequences of distress risk has examined governance changes
emanating from ﬁnancial distress (Gilson and Vetsuypens, 1993).

ﬁnancial distress is voluminous and has been published in journals spanning
across a number of disciplines. We adapt Schweizer and Nienhaus’s (2017),
Brauer’s (2006) and Haleblian et al.’s (2009) approaches to selecting papers for
review by identifying, reviewing, and classifying relevant works. First, by

2. Measurement of distress risk

   We summarise some of the distress risk measures used in the existing literature.
This is important because the validity of the reported ﬁndings in the distress
literature must be judged against the reliability of the measurement techniques
applied. Financial statement-based distress indicators were fairly commonly used
up to as late as early 2000. Beaver’s (1966) was the ﬁrst comprehensive study that
identiﬁed 30 ﬁnancial ratios, comprising six groups of ratios in a univariate setting
and concluded that each of the six ratio groups had signiﬁcant explanatory power
relative to a simple model of random prediction. Altman (1968) improved upon
Beaver’s (1966) study by applying multiple discriminant analysis (MDA) and a
much smaller set of ratios: (i) a liquidity measure; (ii) a book leverage measure (iii)
a cumulative proﬁtability measure; (iv) a market leverage measure; and, ﬁnally,
(v) an asset turnover measure.
   Although Altman’s sample consisted of bankrupt as well as non-bankrupt
ﬁrms, many subsequent studies calculated a Z-score for the entire sample group
to predict certain outcomes, for example, earnings management. Both Beaver
and Altman relied on reported numbers in the ﬁnancial statements to develop
their prediction score. Although intuitive, this approach suﬀers from some
inherent limitations as outlined by Beaver et al. (2010, p. 114).

    Historical ﬁnancial statements do not provide direct measures of the expectation or
    variability of net asset ﬂows from operations. Moreover, the act of defaulting on a
    debt obligation or declaring bankruptcy is likely based upon multiperiod
    considerations, such as the expected value and variability of net asset ﬂows from
    operations over several periods. In a multiperiod setting, default or bankruptcy can
    occur prior to the cash balance equalling zero.
   In order to overcome the limitations associated with an accounting-based
distress prediction model,1 researchers have developed market value-based
prediction models. This approach draws from the Merton (1974) model and the
option pricing theory of Black and Scholes (1973). In these models, equity is
valued as a European call option on the value of the ﬁrm’s assets. The key
variables in the model are the asset value, asset volatility, face value of debt,
risk-free rate and risk premium on equity. Table 1 summarises some of the key
distress calculation measures used in empirical research, along with the number
of surveyed papers (both the determinants and consequences of ﬁnancial
distress) using each measure.2
   It is evident from Table 1 that the majority of the research has used, and
continues to use, accounting-based measures such as Altman (1968), Ohlson’s
(1980) and the Zmijewski (1984) scores, as well as the more rudimentary
measures of negative working capital, negative operating cash ﬂows (OCF) and
interest coverage ratio. Yet more sophisticated market-based measures, such as
BSM-Prob, distance-to-default (DTD) and CHS (Campbell et al., 2008), are
rarely used. It seems that the mainstream accounting and ﬁnance research still
consider the ability of ﬁnancial statements to assess ﬁnancial distress as
important, despite inherent limitations, such as their use of historical costs,
failure to capture intangible assets (R&D) and expected future volatility, and
limited scope. This may be because ﬁnancial distress oﬀers a context in which to
evaluate the usefulness of ﬁnancial statements: a focal point and key concern to
policy-makers, standard setters and accounting professionals (Beaver et al.,
2010). For instance, Bodle et al. (2016) ﬁnd that International Financial
Reporting Standards (IFRS) rules for intangibles can better predict corporate
bankruptcies in Australia.

and market-based distress risk measures to investigate the possible determi-

measures are backward looking and, hence, may prove less relevant for
predicting distress. Despite this limitation, the bulk of the literature appears to
rely on historical accounting-based measures to proxy for ﬁnancial distress.
Stronger inferences can be obtained if researchers can incorporate both
accounting and market-based measures, and assess the relative superiority of
one measure over another.

3. Determinants of financial distress

  In this section, we review the literature on the determinants of ﬁnancial
distress. We organise our review by categorising the determinants into (i) ﬁrm-
level fundamental determinants; (ii) macroeconomic determinants; and (iii)
corporate governance determinants. These categories are not mutually exclu-
sive; rather, much richer insights may be gained by incorporating possibly all
three categories in a study.

3.2. Macroeconomic determinants of ﬁnancial distress

  It is intuitive to predict that ﬁnancial distress risk increases during periods of
economic recession owing to declining sales, cash ﬂows and business proﬁtability.
The economic condition of a country also impacts upon the business environment
through changes in inﬂation rates, interest rates, employment rates, credit
availability and monetary policy (Liou and Smith, 2007). Research suggests that
incorporating country risk factors into ﬁrm-level distress prediction models
improves their explanatory power (Tinoco and Wilson, 2013; Altman et al., 2017),
which is consistent with the evidence that macroeconomic variables account for
nearly half of the variation in ﬁrms’ earnings and earnings changes (Chordia and
Shivakumar, 2005; Bonsall et al., 2013). However, the bulk of the studies assume
that all ﬁrms behave in a predictable manner during economic recession or
expansion, despite the intuition that (i) ﬁnancially healthy ﬁrms in an expansion
period could become ﬁnancially distressed during a recessionary period; (ii)
distressed ﬁrms during expansionary periods could plunge further into ﬁnancial
distress, or even become bankrupt during a recessionary period or (iii) ﬁnancially
healthy ﬁrms during expansionary periods could maintain, albeit to a lesser extent,
economic viability. Future research should incorporate these possibilities into the
research design, to oﬀer a better insight into ﬁrms’ response to economic
uncertainties conditional on distress status. Also, the moderating eﬀect of ﬁrm-
level governance variables should be considered when testing for the association
between ﬁnancial distress and economic cycles (Aldamen et al., 2012).
3.3. Corporate governance and ﬁnancial distress

  Corporate governance is a set of mechanisms through which outside
investors protect themselves against expropriation by insiders (Shleifer and
Vishny, 1997). Weak corporate governance creates an opportunity for
controlling shareholders and managers to beneﬁt from a company at the cost
of non-controlling shareholders. Johnson et al. (2000) suggest that corporate
governance explains ﬁnancial crisis better than macroeconomic variables.
Hence, corporate governance has been one of the most discussed topics among
researchers and policy-makers alike, and the role of corporate governance in
mitigating ﬁnancial distress is relevant to our review.
3.3.1. Board structure and ﬁnancial distress

  Boards of directors are at the helm of corporate governance (Hermalin and
Weisbach, 1998; Hermalin and Weisbach, 2003; Adams et al., 2010). The
ultimate consequence of a dysfunctional corporate internal control system,
including an ineﬃcient and ineﬀective corporate board, is the failure of the ﬁrm
(Simpson and Gleason, 1999). A number of board characteristics have been
examined having implications for ﬁnancial distress, although the ﬁndings
remain mixed.

3.3.2. CEO characteristics and ﬁnancial distress

  Kallunki and Pyykko (2013) argue that ‘. . .since corporate decisions are
taken by individuals, the likelihood of subsequent ﬁnancial distress is aﬀected
not only by ﬁrm characteristics but also by the personal characteristics of
management’ (p. 299). Using past defaulting history of the CEOs and directors
for a sample of private ﬁrms in Finland, they provide robust evidence that
default characteristics increase the probability of ﬁnancial distress. Ho et al.
(2016) ﬁnd that banks with overconﬁdent CEOs suﬀered more during the GFC
period compared to the banks with not-so overconﬁdent CEOs. This stream of
research provides avenues for further research: For example, managerial ability
(Demerjian et al., 2013), CEO social ties and CEO positional embeddedness
(Bhabra and Eissa, 2017), can be included as additional CEO characteristics
into the distress prediction models. The inclusion of CEOs having social ties
with other CEOs who have been designated as problem CEOs, may also allow
for tests of contagion eﬀects on ﬁnancial distress.11

females are more risk-averse (Powell and Ansic, 1997), less conﬁdent
(Jianakoplos and Bernasek, 1998; Bonner, 2008) and more compliant with
regulations (Baldry, 1987) than their male counterparts. Hence, ﬁrms with male

3.3.3. Ownership structure and ﬁnancial distress

  Whether a large block ownership (a proxy for ownership concentration)
exacerbates or attenuates the risk of ﬁnancial distress is debated. Studies that
ﬁnd beneﬁcial eﬀects of large block holdings in mitigating distress include
Barclay and Holderness (1989); Gilson (1990); Elloumi and Gueyie (2001);
Parker et al. (2002); Miglani et al. (2015). The beneﬁcial eﬀect arises because
large block-holders have strong incentives to constrain managerial actions, for
example, excessive risk-taking, that might increase ﬁnancial distress. Lee and
Yeh (2004) document a positive association between large controlling share-
holdings and ﬁnancial distress, probably owing to the collusion of a large
shareholder with management to maximise private control beneﬁts (Johnson
et al., 2000; La Porta et al., 2000). Wang and Deng (2006) and Li et al. (2008)
ﬁnd a negative relationship between state ownership and corporate ﬁnancial
distress in China and suggest that ‘Survival with poor performance’ is a
common phenomenon among Chinese State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs).
  One distinguishing form of block-holder ownership is family ownership.
Family owned ﬁrms are characterised by pursuit of interests and goals that are
often none-economic, such as the emotional attachment of the family to the
business (Gomez-Mejia et al., 2011), desire of the family to preserve the
business in the long run and ultimately passing it on to next generations
(Anderson and Reeb, 2003; Berrone et al., 2012). As a result, the investment
strategies of family ﬁrms are often long term in nature, focusing on ﬁrm
survival rather than short-term proﬁt maximisation. Gottardo and Moisello
(2017) conﬁrm that Italian family ﬁrms are less likely to encounter ﬁnancial
distress than non-family ﬁrms. Family ﬁrms have strong social ties so that the
ﬁrms closely bind with their stakeholders, who may be able to provide some
ﬂexibility in meeting obligations when the ﬁrms are in ﬁnancial diﬃculties
(Berrone et al., 2012). However, the presence of numerous family members on
a board can aﬀect adversely a family ﬁrm’s survival probability (Le Breton-
Miller et al., 2011).

distress, too, is inconclusive. A considerable body of literature indicates that
institutional investors are important monitoring agents and, typically, exercise an
active role in improving ﬁrm performance and ﬁnancial reporting quality

4. Consequences of financial distress

   We now review the literature that investigates the consequences of distress
risk. A wide variety of distress outcomes have been explored in the accounting
and ﬁnance literature. The former focuses on ﬁnancial reporting, auditing and
ﬁrm-level operational consequences of ﬁnancial distress (Sections 4.1 and 4.2):
the latter on capital market consequences (Section 4.3). A further stream of
research has investigated the corporate governance consequences of ﬁnancial
distress (Section 4.4).

failed companies during the 1977–1986 period and ﬁnd that 39 percent (12
percent) of the companies with a high (low) probability of ﬁnancial distress
received a going-concern audit opinion. The advent of ﬁnancial crisis

   A stream of academic research has argued that higher business risk, proxied
by ﬁnancial distress, aggravates auditors’ litigation risk and requires auditors to
exert more audit eﬀort, thus, increasing both audit fees (Jubb et al., 1996;
Niemi, 2002), and audit report lag (ARL) for distressed ﬁrms (Whittred and
Zimmer, 1984; Carslaw and Kaplan, 1991). But Xu et al. (2013) do not ﬁnd any
such evidence, probably owing to client pressure on auditors to release audited
annual reports quickly, as many debt covenants are based on accounting
ﬁgures. Also, the adoption of new accounting standards in 2005 and 2006 (pre-
GFC period) led to longer ARL in those years, which subsequently reduced, as
clients, accountants and auditors became familiar with the standards from 2007
onwards (i.e. post-GFC period). Bhaskar et al. (2017) ﬁnd that auditors charge
higher audit fees and render more going-concern opinions, but experience a
greater likelihood of resignation for ﬁrms with debt covenant violations than
for ﬁrms with no such violations. However, the study remains silent on
auditors’ choice to audit distressed versus non-distressed ﬁrms, which is
surprising considering the reputation and litigation risks associated with the
ultimate demise of some of the distressed violators. Gul et al. (2018) ﬁnd that
ﬁnancially distressed ﬁrms with more able managers exhibit poor accrual
quality and a higher likelihood of restatement: an observation that might
explain the weaker negative association between managerial ability and audit
fees for distressed ﬁrms. Hudaib and Cooke (2005) ﬁnd that ﬁnancially
distressed companies having CEO changes are the most likely to receive a
qualiﬁed audit opinion. Du and Lai (2018) examine the existence of the
contagion eﬀect14 of low audit quality in China and ﬁnd that the eﬀect is more
pronounced for ﬁnancially distressed clients, than for their non-distressed
counterparts. These ﬁndings have important implications to investors, among
many other stakeholders, because they provide an early warning when assessing
the earnings quality of other ﬁrms audited by the same auditor.

4.2. Financial distress and ﬁrm-level operational consequences

4.2.1. Tax avoidance

  A distressed ﬁrm may choose tax planning as an added tool to overcome
distress. Richardson et al. (2015a) ﬁnd that distressed ﬁrms engage in more tax
avoidance compared to their healthy ﬁrm counterparts in Australia, consistent
with the argument that tax savings may inject much-needed cash for survival.
Importantly, this eﬀect was more pronounced during the GFC period. This is
consistent with the argument that credit-constrained ﬁrms during the GFC
might have used tax avoidance as an alternative source of ﬁnance for their
operations. Richardson et al. (2015b) also conﬁrm this ﬁnding using US data.
They also ﬁnd that the interaction between board independence and ﬁnancial
distress is associated with tax aggressiveness positively. Although the ﬁndings
are consistent with the argument that distressed ﬁrms avoid tax to divert cash
from the government to the ﬁrms, no empirical evidence yet exists to suggest
that this is indeed the case. Furthermore, research on the extent to which
diﬀerent stages of ﬁnancial distress (see 4.1.1. above) moderate the association
between ﬁnancial distress and tax avoidance can oﬀer interesting insights.

4.2.2. Financial distress and market forces

  There has been increasing interest in the accounting and ﬁnance ﬁeld in
understanding the impact of ﬁnancial distress on some key components of
market forces, as identiﬁed in Porter’s industry and competitive analysis model
(Porter, 1980, 1985).15 Molina and Preve (2009) examine trade receivables
policies by ﬁnancially distressed ﬁrms. Distressed ﬁrms with cash ﬂow problems
may reduce the trade credit terms. This inference is evidenced in the more
concentrated industries, where ﬁrms generally have greater market power. Such
ﬁrms initiate larger reductions in trade receivables to their customers when
facing ﬁnancial distress. However, distressed ﬁrms with larger reductions in
trade receivables experience an extra 13 to 20 percent drop in sales: a trend that
might trigger further distress. Molina and Preve (2012) examine the use of
supplier’s trade credit by ﬁrms in ﬁnancial distress and ﬁnd that these ﬁrms use
a signiﬁcantly larger amount of trade credit. However, given the high costs of
trade credit, ﬁrms that have increased their trade payable experience more than
one-third decreases in operating income and sales.

health of the supplier ﬁrms. Using both Altman (1968) and the option
pricing model of Merton (1974), Lian (2017) documents that a supplier’s

customer–supplier relationship, or the competitive industrial relationship.
We, therefore, suggest future study on capital market reactions to the
expected performance of non-distressed competitors and/or suppliers. When
the market learns about a ﬁrm’s distress status through debt covenant
violations, for example, their non-distressed competitors may suﬀer a
contagion eﬀect in terms of a higher cost of equity capital.

4.3. Capital market consequences of ﬁnancial distress

  Researchers on ﬁnancial distress in ﬁnance have investigated dividend
adjustment, reorganisation strategies and market anomalies as the three
primary capital market consequences of ﬁnancial distress.

4.3.1. Dividend adjustments

  Firms in protracted ﬁnancial distress are likely to face binding debt
covenants, and the adjustment of dividend payouts may be a tool for easing
covenant violations (Giroux and Wiggins, 1984; Lau, 1987). DeAngelo and
DeAngelo (1990) ﬁnd support for this hypothesis. Their sample consists of
80 troubled NYSE sample ﬁrms, for which they document aggressive and
rather quick dividend reductions in response to ﬁnancial distress. DeAngelo
and DeAngelo’s (1990) sample was limited to ﬁrms with at least three annual

4.3.2. Financial distress and restructuring

   Early studies examine the reorganisation strategies, for example, leveraged
buyouts (LBO), in relation to ﬁnancial distress/bankruptcy costs. Opler and
Titman (1993) ﬁnd that LBO decreases with an increase in expected distress
cost. Lasfer et al. (1996), on the other hand, conﬁrm that divestments by
ﬁnancially distressed ﬁrms in the UK receive more positive market reactions
and, therefore, more value-generating, than the divestment announcements
made by healthy ﬁrms.
   Another form of restructuring strategy is debt restructuring, whereby
distressed ﬁrms can ease creditors’ control, by an injection of fresh money, a
reduction or deferral of contractual payments, or a debt-for-equity swap
(Gilson et al., 1990; Jostarndt and Sautner, 2008). Jostarndt and Sautner
(2008) document a gradual shift from internal to external mechanisms of
corporate ownership and control over a ﬁrm’s distress cycle. Using a sample of
German distressed ﬁrms, they document a signiﬁcant decrease in ownership
concentration over the distress cycle. This change was reﬂected in a reduction in
shareholding from private investors, a substantial increase in senior manage-
ment shareholding, and a doubled ownership represented by banks and outside
investors. In contrast, Gu (2017) documents that US ﬁrms in the high distress
risk quintile have higher ownership concentration but lower institutional
ownership. As to the question of ‘who gets picked’ for a merger, the ineﬃcient
management rationale suggests that distressed ﬁrms with low managerial
eﬃciency have greater potential to recover after acquisition. Once an
‘ineﬃciently managed’ distressed ﬁrm is acquired, its market value could be
increased through much more eﬃcient management (Theodossiou, 1993).
Theodossiou et al. (1996) examine the speciﬁc features of ﬁnancially distressed
ﬁrms that attract more interest in the acquisition. They indeed ﬁnd that
distressed ﬁrms with low managerial eﬃciency are more likely to be acquired.

4.3.3. Financial distress, market pricing and anomalies

   Compared to earnings information, cash ﬂow information is more useful
in explaining the stock returns of ﬁnancially distressed ﬁrms (Casey and
Bartczak, 1985; Chan and Chen, 1991). External ﬁnancing may not be easily
accessible by such ﬁrms, and, consequently, distressed ﬁrms have to rely on
internal cash ﬂows to fund operations. Lee et al. (2017) ﬁnd operating cash
ﬂows, rather than earnings, are much more strongly related to stock returns
when ﬁrms are in ﬁnancial distress. One reason could be the incentives for
distressed ﬁrms to engage in earnings manipulation (see section 4.1.1.).
Hiding bad news through earnings manipulation has the adverse eﬀect of
accentuating stock price crash risk (Jin and Myers, 2006). Future research
could explore whether distressed ﬁrms experience more stock price crash risk
at the time of violation of debt covenants, and/or when receiving qualiﬁed
audit opinions.
   Some researchers have investigated whether ﬁnancial distress can explain
some of the accounting and market anomalies. Commonly investigated
anomalies include accruals anomalies17 (Gu, 2017); investment-growth
anomalies (Su, 2016); and momentum anomalies (Agarwal and Taﬄer,
2008). Gu (2017) tests whether the well-documented accrual anomalies can
be explained from a ﬁrm-distress perspective, and ﬁnds that ﬁrms with high
distress risk drive the abnormal returns of the accrual strategy. Su (2016)
argues that distress risk can explain investment-growth anomalies: lower
stock returns for ﬁrms with greater growth in CAPEX. Su (2016) ﬁnds that
ﬁrms with high (low) capital investment have lower (higher) exposure to
systematic distress risk and, thus, lower (higher) expected returns. Thus, the
variation in realised returns can be viewed as compensation for systematic
distress risk. Agarwal and Taﬄer (2008) ﬁnd that most of the momentum is
generated through the returns continuation of poor performers: a ﬁnding
implying that momentum captures ﬁnancial distress risk. This is consistent
with Beaver’s (1968) ﬁnding: ﬁrms that became bankrupt subsequently earned
negative stock returns as early as 4 years prior to bankruptcy, suggesting the
market’s gradual incorporation of the deteriorating ﬁnancial health of a
ﬁrm. However, Simlai (2014) concludes that distress risk cannot explain the
size and value anomalies using market-based distress measures.

4.4. Financial distress and corporate governance

   Chou et al. (2010) use the absence from board meetings as a proxy measure
for outside directors’ work eﬀorts, and document a signiﬁcantly negative
relationship between the meetings attendance rate and ﬁnancial distress. Gilson
(1989) examines the extent of senior management turnover in ﬁnancially
distressed ﬁrms in the United States and ﬁnds that, in a particular year, a
sizable number (52 percent) of sample ﬁrms experience turnover if they are in
default on their debt, bankrupt or privately restructuring their debt to avoid
bankruptcy. There are signiﬁcant labour market penalties for dismissed
managers, as they are not subsequently employed by another listed ﬁrm for
at least 3 years. DeAngelo et al. (1994) conﬁrm Gilson’s (1989) ﬁndings and
document a 46 percent change in senior management positions in the U.S.
Similar evidence has also been found in continental Europe. Using a sample of
German ﬁrms that suﬀered from ﬁnancial distress, Jostarndt and Sautner
(2008) ﬁnd signiﬁcantly higher management turnover for distressed ﬁrms, and
such turnover was also largely initiated by external shareholders and banks.
   Gilson and Vetsuypens (1993) ﬁnd that CEOs who are successful in retaining
their position in a distressed ﬁrm experience salary and bonus cuts. Guo et al.
(2014) ﬁnd that ﬁnancially distressed ﬁrms reduce CEO compensation, intensify
board monitoring and adjust managerial board appointments. The eﬀects of
ﬁnancial distress on CEO compensation are driven mainly by the part of
compensation related to ﬁrms’ performance. Chang et al. (2016) extend this
stream of research to the CEO turnover, by identifying the new CEO and
examining the eﬀects of ex-ante ﬁnancial distress risk on new CEO compen-
sation in the United States. They evidence that new CEOs receive greater
compensation in the presence of more distress risk. Also, the magnitude of the
distress premium is signiﬁcantly higher for young CEOs, owing to distress-
related career concerns. Moreover, Chang et al. (2016) evidence that distress
risk is associated positively with equity-based compensation, rather than with
cash bonuses. Firms that face ﬁnancial distress are likely to suﬀer from cash
constraints and, therefore, an equity-based compensation package is a more
forward-looking and ﬁrm value-additive approach.
   Although these studies provide interesting insights into the managerial
compensation of distressed ﬁrms, future research needs to examine whether
ﬁnancial distress aﬀects the design of rank-order tournaments, an alternative
implicit form of compensation scheme. Tournament theory posits executives as
contestants for promotion, and the large pay disparity between the CEO and
other senior executives is the prize for winning the tournament (Lazear and
Rosen, 1981). Tournament incentives have been found to aﬀect ﬁrm perfor-
mance (Lazear and Rosen, 1981; Kale et al., 2009; Bebchuk et al., 2011),
earnings manipulation (Park, 2017; Zhang et al., 2018) and the cost of equity
capital (Chen et al., 2013), but no study yet exists that conditions tournament
incentives on the distress status of ﬁrms.

  In addition to the aforementioned corporate governance mechanisms,
corporate disclosures are also widely considered an important mechanism for
attenuating information asymmetries and resolving conﬂicts of interest among
various stakeholders. For distressed ﬁrms, information about the source of the
distress and plans for recovery are narrated primarily in the Management
Discussion and Analysis (MD&A) section of the annual reports (Beller, 2003).
Holder-Webb and Cohen (2007) document that, in the ﬁrst year of distress,
distressed ﬁrms, on average, increase MD&A disclosure quality.18 However,
only those ﬁrms eventually recovering from the distress exhibited sustainable
increases in disclosure quality. Rogers and Stocken (2005) document that
managers of ﬁnancially distressed ﬁrms are more likely to disclose optimistic
forward-looking information, to mitigate potential negative responses from the
markets. Future research could examine the readability of the MD&A section
using text-based readability measures (e.g. Bonsall and Miller, 2017) and
examine whether longitudinal changes in readability are related to changes in
the cost of capital for ﬁnancially distressed ﬁrms.

  Table 3 summarises the consequences of distress risk literature, categorised
into four main themes, that is, (i) ﬁnancial reporting and auditing conse-
quences, (ii) ﬁrm-level operational consequences, (iii) capital market conse-
quences and (iv) corporate governance consequences. The general ﬁndings
from this literature suggest that distressed ﬁrms engage in both income-
increasing and income-decreasing accounting choices at diﬀerent stages of
distress. Rendering a qualiﬁed audit opinion and charging higher audit fees
appear to be the primary responses from external auditors for distressed ﬁrms.
With respect to ﬁrm-level operational consequences, research has found that
distressed ﬁrms avoid tax, adjust the amount of trade receivables and/or trade
payables and impose indirect ﬁnancing costs on non-distressed competitors.
Capital market consequences of ﬁnancial distress generally conclude that
distress risk proxies for a number of market anomalies. Finally, corporate
governance consequences of ﬁnancial distress include poor governance
practices by distressed ﬁrms.

the impact of bank ownership, governance and policy factors (e.g. lending

the ﬁnancial reporting quality of distressed ﬁrms is poorer compared to their
non-distressed counterparts, ﬁrms adopt various earnings manipulation tech-
niques at diﬀerent stages of ﬁnancial distress, and auditors issue more adverse
audit opinions and charge higher audit fees for distressed ﬁrms. Our review also
summarises the implications of ﬁnancial distress for various operational issues,
as well as for various capital market consequences. Finally, corporate
governance consequences, such as board-related variables, management
turnover and compensation, and narrative disclosure quality in the MD&A
section, have emerged recurrently in our review.

ﬁrm life cycle, that might aﬀect the relationship between ﬁnancial distress and

versus weak internal control environments, stages of ﬁrms’ distress status and
so forth. Literature still remains silent on auditors’ choice to audit distressed
versus non-distressed ﬁrms. Future research could also bring in macroeconomic
factors, such as the level of social trust, into empirical testing of this
association. Another possible future research area could be looking at whether,
and why, ﬁnancially distressed ﬁrms conduct acquisitions as a diversiﬁcation,

   We believe that future research can make a more meaningful contribution,
by developing more comprehensive models of predicting ﬁnancial distress.
For example, variables like ﬁnancial reporting quality, management quality
and experience, as well as governance, business model factors, innovation
factors, economic context factors, among others, can enrich the distress

analysis’ to a more holistic complex analysis. In particular, there are a wide

practices’, but are yet to be incorporated into the distress prediction model.

association between corporate governance and ﬁnancial distress is condi-

the ﬁnancial health of ﬁrms is temporary or permanent, the early identiﬁ-
cation of ﬁnancial distress is very important, since ﬁnancial distress gives rise
to both direct and indirect costs that are borne by a large group of
